Your first six weeks after birth

What's normal, what helps, and when to call for help. A calm guide to the weeks your body does some of its biggest work.

You've just done something extraordinary. Over the next six weeks your body heals, your hormones shift, and you learn a whole new person. Some doctors call the first 12 weeks the "fourth trimester".2 Most of what you feel is a normal part of recovery. This guide helps you tell the normal from the things that need a doctor or midwife.

When to get help

Call your local emergency number now if you have:

  • Sudden or very heavy bleeding, or passing large clots13
  • Bleeding with feeling faint, dizzy or a racing heartbeat3
  • Chest pain or trouble breathing4
  • A headache that won't go away, or changes in your vision4
  • Thoughts of harming yourself or your baby14

Call your doctor or midwife straight away if you have:

  • A fever of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher14
  • Vaginal discharge that smells bad1
  • Pain, swelling, redness or warmth in your calf, thigh or arm14
  • Stitches or a caesarean wound that are red, hot or oozing1
  • A lump or hardened area in your breast1
  • Severe belly pain that doesn't go away, or extreme swelling of your hands or face4
  • Low mood or worry that stops you coping and doesn't ease after a few days1

If something doesn't feel right, trust yourself and ask. You will never be wasting anyone's time.

Week by week

What to expect, and when

  1. First days
    Bleeding is at its heaviest. Cramps called afterpains are common, especially while breastfeeding.1
  2. Days 3–4
    Your milk usually comes in, and breasts can feel full and tight.1
  3. Around day 4
    "Baby blues" often peak: tears, mood swings, feeling overwhelmed. They usually ease within about two weeks.1
  4. From week 3
    You can ovulate and get pregnant again, even before your first period.1
  5. Weeks 2–6
    Time for your postnatal check-up. Many women have it at 6–8 weeks.12
  6. Weeks 4–6
    General exercise can often begin, after checking with your doctor or midwife.1
  7. Up to ~2 months
    Bleeding gradually becomes lighter and browner until it stops.1

Every recovery is different. These are typical patterns, not deadlines.

Bleeding after birth

Heavy at first, then lighter and browner

After birth, you bleed as your womb shrinks back to its usual size. This bleeding is called lochia. It is heavy at first, then turns brownish and gets lighter. For most women it lasts a few weeks, and it can continue for about two months.13 It may look redder and heavier while you breastfeed, because feeding makes your womb contract.3

Tell your doctor or midwife aboutbleeding that gets heavier instead of lighter, turns bright red again after it had faded, passes large clots, or smells bad.12 Keeping a used pad to show them can help.1

Healing

Afterpains, stitches and your caesarean wound

  • Afterpains feel like period cramps and are strongest while breastfeeding. A heat pack, or simple pain relief such as paracetamol taken about 30 minutes before a feed, can help.1
  • Stitches are usually dissolvable. Keep the area clean and dry, rinse with plain warm water, and pat dry. Lying on your side takes the pressure off.1
  • Ice wrapped in a cloth can ease swelling.1
  • Ibuprofen and paracetamol are the usual first choices for pain and are considered safe while breastfeeding. Avoid aspirin if you are breastfeeding.2

Breasts & feeding

When your milk comes in

Around day three or four, your breasts may feel full, warm and tight.1 A supportive bra, warm showers, gentle massage, and warm or cold compresses can help.1 However you feed your baby, you deserve support with it.2

Get support forcracked or damaged nipples, signs of mastitis, or a lump or hardened area in your breast.1 A lactation consultant or child health nurse can help.

Bladder, bowel & pelvic floor

Gentle care for the muscles that carried your baby

Passing urine may sting at first. Drinking more water and sitting in warm water can help.1 Try not to strain on the toilet. Fibre, fluids, and a squatting position (feet raised on a low stool) all make it easier.1

Start your pelvic floor exercisesGently squeeze and lift the muscles you use to hold in urine and wind, then relax. Aim for at least 25 squeezes spread through the day.1 They help with leaking when you laugh, cough or sneeze.

Feelings

Baby blues, or something more?

Common and short-lived

Baby blues

  • Tearful, emotional, easily overwhelmed
  • Often peaks around day 41
  • Usually eases within about two weeks1
Talk to someone

When it may be more

  • Low mood or worry that stops you coping and doesn't ease after a few days1
  • Any thoughts of harming yourself or your baby: get help now14

Depression and anxiety after birth can happen to anyone, and help is available. Your doctor may ask you to fill in a short questionnaire about your mood. It is a routine part of postnatal care.2 Partners can struggle too, so check in on each other.2

Rest & food

Rest is part of your recovery

Tiredness is the most common complaint after birth.2 Sleep when your baby sleeps, accept offers of meals and help, keep visitors short, and let the housework wait.1 If tiredness feels overwhelming, mention it to your doctor. Low iron, low mood and thyroid problems can all cause it.2

Foods that help you healProtein, iron-rich foods eaten with vitamin C, calcium from dairy and leafy greens, and plenty of fibre and fluids. Keep water within reach while you feed.1 If you were taking an iodine supplement, ask whether to continue it while breastfeeding.1

Moving again

Start gently and build slowly

  • From day one: gentle breathing, leg movements and pelvic floor exercises.1
  • Around 4–6 weeks: general exercise can often begin. Check with your doctor or midwife first, especially after a caesarean.1
  • Before harder exercise: see a women's health physiotherapist.1 Running is not advised before about 3 months after birth.5
Ease off if you noticepain or a feeling of heaviness in the vagina. It can mean you are doing too much too soon.1

Sex & contraception

In your own time, with one thing to know

There is no set date to have sex again. Wait until you feel ready, which for many women takes weeks or months. Breastfeeding hormones can cause dryness, and a lubricant can help.1

You can get pregnant before your first periodOvulation can happen as early as three weeks after birth.1 If you don't want another pregnancy yet, talk to your doctor early about contraception that suits you and your feeding.2

Check-ups

Your postnatal check is for you, too

Plan a check-up with your doctor or midwife between two and six weeks after birth. Timing varies by country, and many women go at six to eight weeks.12 It is a chance to talk about your healing, bleeding, mood, feeding, sex and contraception, and any leaking or heaviness.12

If you had a pregnancy complicationHigh blood pressure in pregnancy: have your blood pressure checked within about a week of birth, and again at your check-up.2
Gestational diabetes: ask about a glucose test at around six weeks. It raises your future risk of type 2 diabetes, so regular checks matter.2
